    Task 0 · 2 min · Not scored

    Sit, settle, and find your reach

    What this tells me: how far your right hand opens without strain. That decides how wide your first right-hand chord shapes can be. The left hand skips this check. For now, its shapes stay within a 4th (like C to F).

    1. Sit on the front half of the bench, in line with D4 or E4. Keep your feet flat. Your forearms should be about level with the keys. Your elbows sit a little in front of your body.
    2. Let your arms hang at your sides. Then bring your hands to the keys in that same shape. Curved, not clawed.
    3. Right hand: thumb on C4. Don't stretch hard. See which marked key your little finger reaches easily.
    4. Left hand: no reach test. Rest it on C3–F3 with fingers 5-4-3-2. Notice if anything aches while it rests.
    Right-hand reachthumb on C4
    Left-hand resting positionfingers 5-4-3-2, thumb off

    Task 1 · 3 min · Steady beat

    Keep time when the click goes away

    What this tells me: whether you keep the beat yourself or lean on the click. It also shows how natural a click on 2 and 4 feels. Your sense of time from guitar should carry over. This checks that it does at the piano.

    All three use right-hand finger 3 on C4. Start each note from the top of the key, with a loose wrist. Loud or soft doesn't matter.

    1aGap click♩ = 72 · 4 bars of click, 4 bars silent, repeating
    Play quarter notes through 2 full rounds. When the click comes back, listen for where you land.
    1bClick on 2 and 4♩ = 96 · count 1 2 3 4 aloud for the first bar
    Play quarter notes for 16 bars. The clicks are the backbeat, on beats 2 and 4.
    1cSwung eighths on one note♩ = 96 · click on 2 and 4

    The eighths are written even but played swung, long-short. Big-band parts marked "Swing" work the same way. Use the same click as 1b. Hear it first if you like.

    Task 2 · 4 min · Both hands

    Four fingers, each hand, then both

    What this tells me: how even your fingers are, especially 4 and 5. It also shows where your muscles get tight, and whether your hands stay together. In parallel motion, both hands move the same way. In contrary motion, they move in opposite directions. Parallel motion uses different fingers in each hand at the same moment, so it's the harder one. Both patterns use 4 fingers per hand, so the left thumb never plays.

    1. Click on every beat at ♩ = 72. One note per click.
    2. 2a right hand alone, twice. Then left hand alone, twice. Then hands together, twice.
    3. 2b hands together, twice. The left hand starts with finger 2 and walks down.
    4. Play 2c only if 2a felt easy. It uses eighth notes at ♩ = 60.
    5. Stop after 2 clean runs, or as soon as it stops feeling easy.
    2aParallel motionan octave apart · left thumb off · ♩ = 72
    2bContrary motionright thumb on C4, left finger 2 on C3 · ♩ = 72
    2cParallel motion in eighthsoptional · ♩ = 60

    How it went Even: same loudness and spacing. Tight: gripping, or a tight wrist or forearm.

    Task 3 · 3 min · Touch and balance

    Melody over a held left hand

    What this tells me: whether you can already play a melody louder than the chords under it. It also shows how it feels to hold a light left-hand shape. Balance is the first thing that makes simple piano sound like music.

    1. Left hand alone: play each 2-note shape with fingers 5 and 2. Hold it for its full length. Notice if your hand grips while it holds.
    2. Hands together, both at an even mezzo-forte (medium loud).
    3. Again, with the melody in front. The right hand sings. The left hand plays pianissimo (very soft) underneath. Let your weight sink into the right-hand keys. The left hand barely goes down.
    4. Optional: reverse it. Left hand clearly louder, melody soft.
    3Melody over guide tonesoriginal exercise · ♩ = 72 · no pedal

    Playback shows the balance to aim for. The left hand is about half as loud as the right. The left hand plays guide tones, close together: E and A (3rd and 6th) for C6, F and B (7th and 3rd) for G7. Each one moves by step into the next. Barry Harris's own chord shapes come later in the course.

    Task 4 · 5 min · Reading

    Read it once, keep going

    What this tells me: how fast you read in each clef and with both hands. That decides how much of each lesson I write out, and how much I teach by ear.

    1. Pick a tempo slow enough that you won't need to stop. Even ♩ = 40 is fine. Set it on the click bar at the top.
    2. Count 1 bar in, then play each example once. Keep the beat going through mistakes. Look 1 beat ahead.
    3. Rate it and write down the tempo you used. Then hear it if you want to check.
    4. Stop at the first example that feels like puzzling out each note. That tells me what I need to know.
    4aTreble clef, right handC major · quarter and half notes
    4bBass clef, left handF major
    4cTreble clef, eighth notesB♭ major · 1 accidental (a natural) · play even, not swung
    4dHands togetherG major
    4eStretch goal: Bach, Invention No. 1 in C major, BWV 772, mm. 1–2optional · right hand first · ♩ = 50 or slower

    These are Bach's notes as they appear in the Peters edition you sent. Ornaments (trills and turns), fingerings and the editor's markings are left off. The ♩ = 50 is a practice tempo, not Bach's. His manuscript gives no tempo. If the right hand feels comfortable, try m. 1 hands together. The left hand comes in on beat 3 and needs no thumb (fingers 5-4-3-2-4-3-5).

    Task 5 · 3 min · Ear to keys

    Hear it, sing it, find it

    What this tells me: how fast what you hear turns into the right keys. On guitar, you've done this many times. On piano, you have to build it again. How far along you are decides how much I teach by ear.

    1. Play the phrase twice. Notes stay hidden.
    2. Sing it back once.
    3. Find it on the piano with your right hand. Start from the marked note. Keep it in time if you can.
    4. Then show the notes to check.

    By ear, play the first 4 bars of a head you know well. A head is the main melody of a jazz tune. A blues head you've played on guitar works well. Don't look it up first.

    Task 6 · Wrap-up

    Answer 6 questions and send it

    What happens next: I set your baseline and write the 12-week plan. I also write the first 7 lessons and put the sessions on your calendar.

    Over the whole session

    The 6 questions Short answers are fine. You can also answer in chat.

    Is it acoustic or digital? What make and model? Are the keys weighted, and which pedals does it have? What do you use at the piano: an iPad or a laptop? Can the piano connect by USB-MIDI?
    How do you read treble and bass clef: note by note, slow but steady, or easily? Have you played anything hands together yet?
    Any discomfort in your hands, wrists, arms, shoulders or neck? Any past injuries, or tight-muscle habits from guitar? Anything to avoid?
    What's your start date, and is Eastern time right? When can you practice on weekdays and weekends? Should I use a new "Piano" calendar (you create it, I fill it) or your main one? May I check your calendars for clashes?
    Which tracks by Powell, Silver or Clark do you love? Which Bach piece would you like to play someday? How should your time split between jazz and Bach? My default is about 70/30.
    Can you send short phone videos, audio or MIDI? For the app: which GitHub account or organization? The same Vercel account? One practice device, or several?
